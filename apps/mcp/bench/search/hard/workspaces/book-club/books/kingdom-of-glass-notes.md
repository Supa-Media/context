# Kingdom of Glass notes

Reading notes for the book club's pick for the autumn, kept chapter by chapter. The discussion is on the second Tuesday. These notes are for anyone who wants to skim before the meeting; nothing here is a spoiler for the first half of the book, which the group agreed to read first.

## Part one: the harbour (chapters 1 to 6)

The book opens on the harbour, where the glass works are the town's main employer. Mara comes back after eleven years away, and the first pages are about the smell of the kilns and the sound of the bell. The author spends a long time on the small things: the cracked step outside the chandler's, the boy who sells bread from a cart, the way the tide stops the mill.

The group agreed the opening was slow but that it paid off. The world of the book is built by the details, and the reader is meant to know the harbour before anything goes wrong in it. Three people said they wanted to skip ahead and then went back.

## Part two: the glass (chapters 7 to 13)

The glass works come into focus. The master, Cass, is a sharp and tired woman who knows everything about the furnace and nothing about her own family. Her son Ilan is the apprentice, and most of the tension in these chapters comes from the wages dispute, which is only ever described from the mill side.

Chapter nine has a long argument about whether the furnace should be rebuilt or patched, and it is the chapter most people found tedious. It is worth reading for chapter ten, where Cass finally tells Ilan why she never went to the city.

## Part three: the sluice (chapters 14 to 21)

The middle of the book is a set of short chapters that alternate between the glass works and the upper town. The hinge scene is in chapter fourteen, where the iron hinge of the workshop door breaks in the night and Mara finds the broken half in the water. The author uses the hinge twice more, and it is a useful thing to track.

The group liked the upper-town chapters less. Ansel, the councillor's son, is a flat character until chapter nineteen, when his debts come out, and after that he is much more interesting. Most people guessed where he was going before he got there.

## Part four: the reckoning (chapters 22 to 30)

The last part is quick. In chapter twenty-two, Ansel betrays the harbour by opening the sluice gate at the top of the tide, which floods the lower works and ends the wages dispute in the worst possible way. The chapter is short and everyone agreed it was the best scene in the book, because the reader is told what happened in a single line of the councillor's diary before it is shown.

After that the book is about the aftermath. Mara stays in the harbour and rebuilds the furnace with Cass, and the last chapter returns to the broken hinge, which she has kept in her coat pocket the whole time.

## Things to ask at the meeting

- Was the middle part too long, or was the slow pace the point?
- Did Ansel's betrayal feel earned, or was it a twist for its own sake?
- Who would you cast as Cass?
- Did the hinge stand for anything, or was it just a hinge?

## Rating

The group gave the book three and a half stars. The main complaint was the wages dispute, which was always offstage. The main praise was the sluice chapter and the last line.

## Characters

Mara is the outsider who returns, and the book is at its best when she is still unsure whether she belongs in the harbour. Cass is the master of the glass works, the one who knows the furnace, and the reader sees her only through what other people say about her. Ilan is the apprentice, the one with the most to lose in the wages dispute, and he is written with more patience than most of the other characters.

Ansel is the councillor's son who turns out to be the hinge of the whole plot, although the author never says so. The group thought the councillor himself was underwritten. He appears in four scenes and speaks in only two, which makes the upper town feel like a rumour.

The minor characters are the strongest part of the book. The bread boy from the first chapter comes back in the last part, and his reappearance is the only moment anybody laughed at the meeting, because the book had taken itself so seriously up to then.

## Themes

The book is about inheritance, though the word is never used. Mara inherits the furnace from a father she barely knew, Ilan inherits the wages dispute from his mother, and Ansel inherits a debt that his father would rather no one read about. Each of them tries to pay it off in a different way, and the glass is the thing that either breaks or holds.

The glass itself is the other theme. Everything in the harbour is made of it or sold by the people who make it, and the book keeps returning to the way glass shows you the room and also what is behind you. Several readers thought the glass was overdone. Others thought the repetition was the point.

## Setting

The harbour is real in the sense that every reader can picture it, and it is invented in the sense that nobody could find it on a map. The author describes the weather in each chapter, and the weather tracks the mood of the glass works. The storms come in the middle part and the calm returns after the sluice chapter.

## Points for the host

Start with the hinge, because it is the easiest thing to talk about and everybody remembers it. Then ask about the sluice chapter, which divides the group more than anything else. Finish with the last line, which the group agreed was the right way to end even if it was a little tidy.

## Chapter by chapter, quick reference

- Chapter 1: Mara arrives on the night ferry; the bell is the first sound.
- Chapter 3: the bread boy and the cart; the first mention of the wages list.
- Chapter 6: the tide stops the mill; Cass refuses to let the furnace cool.
- Chapter 10: Cass tells Ilan why she never left the harbour.
- Chapter 14: the iron hinge breaks in the night; half of it is found in the water.
- Chapter 19: Ansel's debts come out in the upper town.
- Chapter 22: the sluice gate is opened at the top of the tide.
- Chapter 30: the hinge is back in Mara's coat pocket.

## What to read if you only read one part

Read chapters 14 and 22 together. The hinge and the sluice are the two hinges of the book, and the middle part makes sense only once the reader has seen what the sluice does to the lower works.
